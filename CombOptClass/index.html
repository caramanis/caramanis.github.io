Combinatorial Optimization

Welcome to this lecture series on Combinatorial Optimization — an advanced graduate course recorded from material taught at The University of Texas at Austin.

This course develops the algorithmic and polyhedral theory of combinatorial optimization. The central theme is the tight interplay between LP relaxations, integrality results, and combinatorial structure: we ask when LP relaxations are exact, build the tools to prove it (total unimodularity, total dual integrality, the ellipsoid method), and apply them to the most important problems in the field.

Prerequisites: A graduate course in linear programming (LP duality, basic polyhedral theory, complementary slackness) and mathematical maturity at the PhD level. Familiarity with graph theory and linear algebra is assumed throughout.

What this course covers: We begin with polytopes, the simplex method, LP duality, and the primal-dual algorithmic framework. We then cover network flows, the ellipsoid algorithm and oracle complexity, bipartite and non-bipartite matching (including the matching polytope and weighted matching), total unimodularity, total dual integrality, matroids and matroid intersection (including the independent set polytope and matroid intersection polytope), submodularity and the continuous greedy algorithm, and extended formulations with communication-complexity lower bounds on extension complexity.

How to use this material:

1. Lectures are designed to be watched in order. The course builds systematically: polyhedral tools developed early reappear throughout, and later modules (matroids, submodularity, extended formulations) rely heavily on the LP and duality foundations.

2. Lecture slides (PDF) are linked below each video. The lectures are blackboard-style, so the slides capture all definitions, theorems, and proofs.

3. Useful references: Schrijver's Combinatorial Optimization: Polyhedra and Efficiency (the definitive reference), Cook, Cunningham, Pulleyblank & Schrijver's Combinatorial Optimization, and Lovász & Plummer's Matching Theory.

Module 1: Introduction

  • Lecture 1.1: Introduction We introduce combinatorial optimization — the study of graphs, paths, cuts, subsets, assignments, and schedules — and explain the course’s central approach: Algorithms, Geometry, and Polyhedral Techniques. The key insight motivating the course is that LP (min c^Tx s.t. Ax≤b) is a subclass of convex optimization whose optimal solutions sit at extreme points (‘corners’) of the feasible polytope; these corners can encode combinatorial structure, so cleverly designed LP relaxations let us solve hard discrete problems efficiently. The course is organized in five parts: Part I (Linear Optimization) covers the geometry of polyhedra, LP duality, and algorithms (simplex, ellipsoid, primal-dual); Part II (Polyhedral Combinatorics) covers Total Unimodularity, Total Dual Integrality, bipartite and non-bipartite matching, and matroids with their polytopes; Part III (Submodularity) covers definitions, continuous extensions, and algorithms; Part IV covers Extended Formulations; Part V covers Randomized Rounding and other topics. Prerequisites are linear algebra, optimization and duality, and mathematical maturity.

Module 2: Polytopes and the Simplex Method

  • Lecture 2.1: Polytopes and Extreme Points We develop the polyhedral framework that underlies the entire course. We define hyperplanes H={x∈ℝⁿ: a^Tx=b}, half-spaces H+={x∈ℝⁿ: a^Tx≥b}, and polyhedra P={x∈ℝⁿ: Ax≥b} — all convex sets — and note that a bounded polyhedron is a polytope. The main theorem gives three equivalent characterizations of extreme points of P: (1) x cannot be written as a convex combination λy+(1−λ)z for any distinct y,z∈P; (2) x is the unique minimizer of some linear objective c^Tx over P; (3) exactly n of the constraints are linearly independent and tight at x (Basic Feasible Solution / BFS). We specialize to standard-form polyhedra P={Ax=b, x≥0} (showing Ax≤b can always be put in standard form via slack variables), where a BFS corresponds to a basis B⊆[n] with |B|=m: the n−m non-basic variables are zero, and x_B = A_B^{-1}b. Two extreme points are adjacent if their bases share m−1 indices. We then prove: if P is bounded, P = conv(Ext P), and consequently, if a linear program over a bounded P is feasible, an optimal solution exists at some extreme point.

  • Lecture 2.2: The Simplex Method, Part I We develop the simplex method as a procedure for moving between adjacent extreme points of the standard-form polyhedron P={Ax=b, x≥0}. Starting at a BFS x=(x_B;0) with basis B, we pick an entering variable î∉B and compute the direction d: d_î=1, d_j=0 for j∉B∪{î}, and d_B = −A_B^{-1}A_î (so that A(x+θd)=b). The new point x_new = x+θd must remain non-negative, giving the ratio test θ = min{j∈B, d_j>0} x_j/d_j; the minimizing index j̃ leaves the basis, giving B_new = (B∖{j̃})∪{î}. We analyze all three cases for θ: (1) θ>0 finite — we move to a strictly adjacent BFS; (2) θ=+∞ — all d_j≤0, meaning the LP is unbounded; (3) θ=0 — a degenerate step in which we change basis but stay at the same vertex. Degeneracy arises when multiple bases describe the same extreme point (x_j=0 for some j∈B). A worked example with m=3, n=7 illustrates a degenerate pivot where θ=0 is forced._

  • Lecture 2.3: The Simplex Method, Part II We introduce reduced costs as the key optimality certificate for simplex. After a step to x_new = x+θd, the change in objective is c^Tx_new − c^Tx = θ·c̄_î where c̄_î := c_î − (c_B^T A_B^{-1})A_î is the reduced cost of the entering variable î. Note c̄_î=0 for î∈B, c̄_î<0 means adding î to the basis might improve the objective (if θ>0), and if c̄≥0 for all non-basic variables then x is optimal. This yields the complete conceptual simplex algorithm: compute reduced costs c̄ = c − c_B^T A_B^{-1}A; if c̄≥0 then x is optimal; else pick any î with c̄_î<0, compute d_B=−A_B^{-1}A_î, θ=min{j:d_j>0} x_j/d_j, and update B. We close with computational considerations: (A) naive simplex can cycle forever on degenerate problems; (B) a simple pivot rule (e.g., always choose smallest index) guarantees finite termination, though worst-case exponential steps are possible; (C) maintaining A_B^{-1} efficiently across pivots is the key implementation challenge (smoothed analysis explains the good practical behavior)._

    Slides (PDF)

  • Lecture 2.4: The Simplex Method, Part III We introduce the simplex tableau as the data structure that makes simplex practical. The key observation is that A_B^{-1}A = [I | A_B^{-1}A{B^c}]: the m×n matrix obtained by premultiplying A by A_B^{-1} has an identity block on the B-columns and encodes d_B = −A_B^{-1}A_î for every possible entering variable î∉B in a single matrix. The full tableau is (m+1)×(n+1) with a 0th row tracking the objective: the top-left entry is −c_B^T A_B^{-1}b (negative of the current value), and the top row is c − c_B^T A_B^{-1}A (the vector of reduced costs). One simplex pivot is then three row operations: (1) divide the pivot row by the pivot element; (2) subtract multiples of the pivot row from all other rows to zero out the pivot column; (3) update the 0th row by adding a multiple of the pivot row to set c̄î = 0. We prove that after this operation the 0th row correctly encodes the new reduced costs c − c{B_new}^T A_{B_new}^{-1}A. A worked example runs the full simplex algorithm on a 3×5 system with slack variables, tracing two pivots (including a degenerate step with θ=0) through the tableau until c̄≥0 and optimality is certified._

Module 3: LP Duality and Primal-Dual Algorithms

  • Lecture 3.1: LP Duality, Part I We build LP duality from two geometric foundations. First, a convex closed set C⊆ℝⁿ equals the intersection of all half-spaces containing it (C=∩{H⊇C}H), and for any x∉C there exists a separating hyperplane. Second, for the general constrained problem min f(x) s.t. x∈𝒳, x̂ is optimal if and only if 0∈∂f(x̂)+N_𝒳(x̂) — the normal cone condition — and the pair (ĝ,n̂) satisfying 0=ĝ+n̂ are the dual variables (KKT conditions). We then derive LP duality via a min-max argument: for min c^Tx s.t. Ax≥b, introduce multipliers p≥0 to form the Lagrangian min_x max_{p≥0} [c^Tx+p^T(b-Ax)]; swapping min and max (valid by strong duality) gives max_{p≥0} min_x [p^Tb+(c-p^TA)x] = max p^Tb s.t. p^TA=c, p≥0. Weak duality (≥) holds by the swap inequality; strong duality (equality) is stated as a fact. We close with a general LP duality table as an exercise: primal equality constraints ↔ free dual variables; primal inequality constraints ↔ nonneg dual variables; primal nonneg variables ↔ dual ≤ constraints; primal free variables ↔ dual equality constraints._

    Slides (PDF)

  • Lecture 3.2: LP Duality, Part II We derive the dual of the standard-form LP (min c^Tx s.t. Ax=b, x≥0) via min-max: introducing free multipliers p yields the dual max p^Tb s.t. p^TA≤c, p free. Weak duality is immediate: for any feasible primal x and dual p, c^Tx≥p^Tb, because c^Tx−p^Tb = (c−p^TA)x+(p^TAx−p^Tb) = (c−p^TA)x≥0. We then prove strong duality using the simplex method: when simplex terminates at an optimal BFS x̂=(x_B;0), set p̂=c_B^T A_B^{-1}. Since c̄=c−p̂^TA≥0, p̂ is dual feasible; and p̂^Tb=c_B^T A_B^{-1}b=c_B^T x_B=c^Tx̂, so the duality gap is zero. The summary of strong duality has four parts: (A) if either primal or dual has an optimal solution, so does the other and optimal values are equal; (B) primal unbounded ⟹ dual infeasible; (C) the simplex algorithm simultaneously produces primal and dual optimal solutions; (D) complementary slackness. We prove complementary slackness: given a primal-dual feasible pair (x,p), they are jointly optimal iff p_i(a_i^Tx−b_i)=0 ∀i and (c_j−p^TAj)x_j=0 ∀j; the proof notes that Σu_i+Σv_j=c^Tx−p^Tb, so both sides zero iff the duality gap is zero.

  • Lecture 3.3: LP Duality, Part III We draw out the primal-dual symmetry hidden inside the simplex tableau. The tableau row c̄=c−c_B^T A_B^{-1}A is exactly c−p̂^TA where p̂=c_B^T A_B^{-1}: primal simplex maintains x_B=A_B^{-1}b≥0 (primal feasibility) and works toward c̄≥0, i.e., dual feasibility p̂^TA≤c. The dual simplex runs this in reverse: it maintains c̄≥0 (dual feasibility) while A_B^{-1}b is not necessarily ≥0 (not primal feasible), and pivots to restore primal feasibility. A basis B with A_B^{-1}b not componentwise ≥0 but c̄≥0 defines a Basic Solution (BS) that is dual-feasible but not a BFS. The dual simplex pivot rule: (1) choose the pivot row by selecting any i with x{B_i}=（A_B^{-1}b)i<0 as the leaving variable; (2) choose the entering column ĵ = argmin{i: v_i<0} c̄i/|v_i| where v is the corresponding row of A_B^{-1}A (the dual ratio test), which maintains c̄≥0 after the pivot. The dual simplex terminates when A_B^{-1}b≥0 (primal feasibility restored) or certifies primal infeasibility when all v_i≥0 for some negative-valued row.

  • Lecture 3.4: Primal-Dual Algorithm, Part I We introduce the primal-dual (PD) algorithmic framework. The main idea is that if (x,p) are primal-dual feasible and complementary slackness (CS) holds, they are optimal. The PD algorithm starts with a dual feasible p and tries to find x satisfying CS; if it cannot, it uses the failure to improve p. The key structure is the admissible set J={j: p^TAj=cj} (the columns for which CS could hold with x_j≥0). The Restricted Primal (RP) attempts to find a primal feasible x supported on J: min Σx^a_i s.t. AJx_J + x^a = b, x_J≥0, x^a≥0 (artificial variables; wlog b≥0). If RP=0 we have found x satisfying CS with p — stop with optimal solution. If RP>0, we solve the Dual Restricted Primal (DRP): max p^Tb s.t. p^TAj≤0 for j∈J, p≤1, p free (note: c absent — DRP is about finding a direction to improve p^Tb without violating existing CS conditions). Let p̄ be the DRP solution. We update p_new = p+θp̄ where θ = min{j∉J: p̄^TAj>0} (cj−p^TAj)/(p̄^TAj), which is the largest step keeping p_new dual feasible. Each update strictly increases p_new^Tb = p^Tb+θ·p̄^Tb._

    Slides (PDF)

  • Lecture 3.5: Primal-Dual Algorithm, Part II We establish the correctness and finiteness of the primal-dual algorithm. Two theorems govern its behavior. Theorem 1 (infeasibility detection): if RP>0 and p̄^TAj≤0 for all j, then the DRP is unbounded, which implies the primal is infeasible. Theorem 2 (admissible set growth): let B be the optimal basis for RP with B⊆J∪[m]; then B∩J⊆J_new — any admissible column that was in the RP’s optimal basis remains admissible after the dual update. The proof uses the fact that if j∈B∩J then in the RP its reduced cost is c̄_j = 0−p̄^TAj = 0, so (p+θp̄)^TAj = cj and j∈J_new. For finiteness: at the chosen θ, a new column j₀ enters J_new (by the minimizing ratio). In the next RP, j₀’s reduced cost is 0−p̄^TAj₀<0, forcing a pivot that strictly reduces the RP objective — guaranteeing finite termination without degeneracy. With degeneracy an anticycling rule together with the finite number of BFS ensures termination. The lecture closes with a preview: the PD method ‘combinatorializes’ our LP — in the next module we apply it to network flow problems, where RP and DRP reduce to simple graph problems.

Module 4: Network Flows

  • Lecture 4.1: Network Flow Problems We introduce network flow problems on a directed graph G=(V,E) with |V|=n nodes. The central data structure is the node-arc incidence matrix A (|V|×|E|): A_ie=+1 if arc e leaves node i, A_ie=−1 if arc e enters node i, and 0 otherwise. Flow conservation at node i is then simply a_i^T f=0. We work through an example showing a 4-node, 5-arc graph and its explicit incidence matrix. We then formulate two problems as LPs. Shortest path (unit flow of minimum cost from s to t): min c^Tf s.t. Af=[1,0,…,0]^T (source row; the t-row is redundant and can be omitted), f≥0. Max flow (maximize total flow from s to t subject to capacities b): max v s.t. Af+dv≤0 (d=−1 at s, +1 at t, 0 elsewhere), f≤b (capacity vector), f≥0. These LP formulations set up the primal-dual analyses in the lectures that follow.

    Slides (PDF)

  • Lecture 4.2: Primal-Dual Applied to Shortest Path We apply the primal-dual framework to the shortest path LP (min c^Tf, Af=[1,0,…,0]^T, f≥0) and derive its dual: max p_s s.t. p_i−p_j≤c_ij ∀(i,j)∈E, p_t=0, p free. The admissible arcs are J={(i,j)∈E: p_i−p_j=c_ij} (the tight dual constraints). The Restricted Primal (RP) sends a unit of artificial flow from s using only arcs in J; the Dual Restricted Primal (DRP) is max p_s s.t. p_i−p_j≤0 ∀(i,j)∈J, p_i≤1, p_t=0, p free. The DRP is a reachability problem: the optimal p̄ assigns p̄_i=1 to nodes reachable from s via arcs in J, p̄_i=0 to nodes from which t is reachable, and 1 to all others. The dual step-size is θ=min{(i,j)∉J, p̄i−p̄_j>0}{c_ij−(p_i−p_j)}, and the minimizing arc becomes tight (enters J) for p_new=p+θp̄. A fully traced 6-step example builds J incrementally until an s-t path exists and the RP value reaches 0. In the closing discussion: defining W={i: t reachable from i using arcs in J}={i: p̄_i=0}, one can show that p_i is fixed once i∈W and equals the shortest-path value from i to t — recovering Dijkstra’s algorithm.

  • Lecture 4.3: Primal-Dual Applied to Max Flow We set up the max flow LP: max v s.t. Af+dv≤0 (d=−1 at s, +1 at t, 0 elsewhere), f≤b, f≥0. Because the capacities ‘b’ appear on the RHS, we want to ‘combinatorialize the RHS,’ so we treat max flow as the dual problem (with f playing the role of the dual variable). Given a dual-feasible f, the admissible set J is the index of tight dual constraints, and the Dual Restricted Primal (DRP) is: max v s.t. Af+dv≤0 (flow conservation), f≤0 for arcs where f=b in D (b is gone from the problem), −f≤0 for arcs where f=0 in D, f≤1, v≤1. Interpreting the DRP: if v=0 then f is optimal (stop). To achieve v=1 there must be an s-t path that uses saturated arcs (f_ij=b_ij) in backward direction (f̄≤0) and zero-flow arcs (f_ij=0) in forward direction (−f̄≤0), and other arcs freely in either direction. This is precisely an augmenting path: an undirected s-t path that uses arc (i,j) in the forward direction only if unsaturated (f_ij<b_ij) and in the backward direction only if f_ij>0; the improved flow is f_new=f+θf̄.

  • Lecture 4.4: Ford-Fulkerson, Part I We develop the LP duality theory for max flow and prove the max-flow min-cut theorem. We define an s-t cut as a subset W⊆V with s∈W and t∈W̄, and its capacity as c(W,W̄)=Σ{(i,j): i∈W, j∈W̄} b_ij. The max flow LP dual is: min Σγ_ij b_ij s.t. p_i−p_j+γ_ij≥0 ∀(i,j)∈E, −p_s+p_t≥1, γ_ij≥0, p free. Given any s-t cut (W,W̄), setting γ_ij=1 if i∈W,j∈W̄ (else 0) and p_i=0 if i∈W (else 1) yields a dual feasible solution with value c(W,W̄); dual feasibility is easy to verify (key point: p_i−p_j+γ_ij is strict when i∈W̄,j∈W since 1−0+0>0). Max-Flow = Min-Cut Theorem: the value of any s-t flow is upper bounded by c(W,W̄) for every s-t cut; moreover, a flow f and cut (W,W̄) are jointly optimal if and only if complementary slackness holds: f_ij=0 for all (i,j)∈E with i∈W̄,j∈W, and f_ij=b_ij for all (i,j)∈E with i∈W,j∈W̄._

  • Lecture 4.5: Ford-Fulkerson, Part II _We give a precise definition of an augmenting path P (given a feasible flow f): a path from s to t in the undirected graph such that each arc (i,j) used in the forward direction satisfies f_ij0. The maximum augmentation along P is Δ=min_{(i,j)∈P}{b_ij−f_ij (forward) or f_ji (backward)}, and f_new=f+Δ. We then present the Ford-Fulkerson labeling algorithm: maintain a LIST of labeled-but-unscanned nodes; initialize with LIST={s} and L_2(s)=+∞. Each node i carries a two-part label (L_1(i), L_2(i)) where L_1 records the predecessor and L_2 records the maximum additional flow routable from s to i. Scanning node i: for each unsaturated forward arc (i,j) with f_ij<b_ij, assign label (L_1(j),L_2(j))=(i, min{b_ij−f_ij, L_2(i)}); for each backward arc (j,i) with f_ji>0, assign (L_1(j),L_2(j))=(−i, min{L_2(i), f_ji}); add newly labeled nodes to LIST and remove i. Stop when LIST=∅. Optimality theorem: if FF terminates with LIST=∅ and t unlabeled, then W=labeled nodes and W̄=unlabeled nodes form an s-t cut satisfying f_ij=b_ij ∀(i,j) with i∈W,j∈W̄ and f_ij=0 ∀(i,j) with i∈W̄,j∈W — precisely the CS conditions — so (W,W̄) is a min cut and f is optimal. Finiteness theorem: when b∈ℚ (rational capacities), FF terminates._

  • Lecture 4.6: Arborescences We open with context: simplex (via tableau) and specialized algorithms like Ford-Fulkerson are ‘not enough’ when the LP has an exponential number of facets — motivating the ellipsoid algorithm. We then develop the r-arborescence problem on digraph D=(V,A) with root r. Definitions: A’⊆A is an r-arborescence if every node v≠r is the head of exactly one arc in A’. C⊆A is an r-cut if C=δ⁻(V’) for some ∅≠V’⊆V \ {r} (inclusion-wise minimal if no proper subset is an r-cut). Blocking theorem: r-arborescences are exactly the inclusion-wise minimal sets intersecting all r-cuts; and minimal r-cuts are the minimal sets intersecting all r-arborescences. The minimum-weight r-arborescence problem is formulated as an IP: min c^Tx s.t. x(δ⁻(W))≥1 ∀∅≠W⊆V \ {r}, x_a∈{0,1} ∀a∈A. Edmonds (1967): the LP relaxation (replacing {0,1} with [0,1]) is exact — its feasible set equals the convex hull of characteristic vectors of r-arborescences. The LP has an exponential number of rows (one per subset of V \ {r}). The separation problem — given y, does y(δ⁻(W))≥1 hold for all W? — reduces to |V|−1 minimum-cut computations (one min r-s cut per s∈V \ {r} treating y as arc capacities via FF). This polynomial separation oracle allows the ellipsoid algorithm (introduced next) to solve the LP in polynomial time.

Module 5: The Ellipsoid Algorithm

  • Lecture 5.1: The Ellipsoid Algorithm, Part I We set up the feasibility problem: given P={x∈ℝⁿ: a_i^Tx≤b_i, i=1,…,m}, assuming P is bounded and (if non-empty) full-dimensional, decide in time polynomial in n and the description lengths ⟨A⟩,⟨b⟩: (A) is P empty? (B) if not, find a point in P. The algorithm uses an oracle: given x, it either certifies x∈P or returns a violated constraint (a_0,b_0). Two initial bounds drive the termination argument: P⊆B(0,R) for R=√n·2^{⟨A,b⟩−n²}; and vol(P)≥2^{−(n+1)⟨A⟩+n³} if P is non-empty. The ellipsoid algorithm initializes E_0=B(0,R²)=E(R²I,0) and at each iteration k queries x_k (the center of E_k) to the oracle: if x_k∈P, stop; otherwise find E{k+1}⊇E_k∩{x: â^Tx≤â^Tx_k}⊇P. A picture shows the ellipsoid being cut by the oracle’s hyperplane; the key requirement is a way to compute E_{k+1} whose volume shrinks quickly enough. We then introduce the Löwner-John ellipsoid: for K⊆ℝⁿ convex, there is a unique minimum-volume ellipsoid E⊇K satisfying K⊇(1/n)E (where (1/n)E is E shrunk by factor n about its center). For a general convex K the LJ ellipsoid may be hard to compute, but for K=E∩H₊ (an ellipsoid intersected with a half-space through its center) it is easy — this is derived in the next lecture._

  • Lecture 5.2: The Ellipsoid Algorithm, Part II We develop the explicit ellipsoid update formula. An ellipsoid E(A,c)={x∈ℝⁿ: (x−c)^T A^{-1}(x−c)≤1} = A^{1/2}S(0,1)+c has volume vol(E(A,c))=√detA·vol(S(0,1)) where vol(S(0,1))=π^{n/2}/Γ(n/2+1). Given 𝒳=E(A,c)∩{x: â^Tx≤â^Tc} (the half-ellipsoid cut through the center), the Löwner-John ellipsoid of 𝒳 is E(Â,ĉ) with: ĉ=c−(1/(n+1))·z, Â=(n²/(n²−1))(A−(2/(n+1))·zz^T), z=(1/√(â^TAâ))Aâ. Volume reduction theorem: vol(Ê)/vol(E) = ((n/(n+1))^{n+1}·(n/(n−1))^{n−1})^{1/2} < e^{−1/(2n)} < 1. The proof first handles the special case E=E(I,0) with a=(-1,0,…,0), giving Â=diag(n²/(n+1)²,n²/(n²−1),…,n²/(n²−1)) and verifying det(Â)=n^{2n}/((n+1)²(n²−1)^{n-1})<exp(−1/n). The general case reduces to this via an invertible affine transformation T mapping E to E(I,0): since vol(T(E_1))/vol(T(E_2))=vol(E_1)/vol(E_2) for any two ellipsoids, the volume ratio is invariant and equals the special case.

  • Lecture 5.3: The Ellipsoid Algorithm, Part III We complete the complexity analysis and put all pieces together. Two lemmas establish the bounding parameters: (1) P={Ax≤b}⊆S(0,R) for R=√n·2^{⟨A,b⟩−n²}, so the initial ball B(0,R²) contains P; (2) if P is full-dimensional, vol(P)≥2^{−(n+1)⟨A⟩+n³}. Combining these with the per-step volume reduction by factor e^{−1/(2n)}: after N=2n((2n+1)⟨A⟩+n⟨b⟩−n³) steps the volume of the current ellipsoid falls below the minimum possible volume of a non-empty P, so we can safely declare P=∅ if no feasible point was found. The complete algorithm: E_0=E(R²I,0) with c_0=0; at each step query c_k to the oracle; if c_k∈P stop and return c_k; else use the oracle’s violated constraint â to compute E{k+1}=(Â_{k+1},c_{k+1}); after k=N steps return ‘P=∅.’ The algorithm only needs the encoding lengths ⟨A⟩,⟨b⟩ to initialize and to set the stopping criterion; it only needs R (circumscribing radius) and r (contained-ball radius). The method as presented is called the ‘central cut’ ellipsoid; related variants include shallow cuts and deep cuts._

  • Lecture 5.4: Strong and Weak Oracles, Part I We introduce the oracle-based complexity framework. Computational complexity is measured as the number of calls to a given oracle plus basic arithmetic operations. For a convex set K⊆ℝⁿ we define: S(K,ε)={x∈ℝⁿ: ‖x−y‖≤ε for some y∈K} (ε-expansion) and S(K,−ε)={x∈K: S(x,ε)⊆K} (ε-deep interior). We then define four problem types in both strong and weak variants. For optimization: Strong Opt (SOPT) — given c∈ℝⁿ, find y∈K maximizing c^Tx over K or assert K=∅; Weak Opt (WOPT) — given c∈ℚⁿ and ε>0, either find y∈ℚⁿ with y∈S(K,ε) and c^Tx≤c^Ty+ε for all x∈S(K,−ε), or assert S(K,−ε)=∅.   • Lecture 5.6: Rational Polyhedra We work with rational polytopes P={Ax≤b} where A_ij, b_i∈ℚ. The punchline stated upfront: if we have upper bounds on the encoding lengths of individual facets or vertices of a polyhedron (as opposed to the full matrix ⟨A⟩,⟨b⟩), then solvability of weak oracle problems implies solvability of strong oracle problems — and we can drop assumptions on full dimensionality and boundedness. We define facet-complexity and vertex-complexity: P has facet-complexity φ if there exists a rational system describing P where each inequality has encoding length ≤φ; P has vertex-complexity υ if P=conv{V}+cone{E} where each vertex v and ray r∈E has encoding length ≤υ (with the convention φ≥n+1 if P=ℝⁿ, υ≥n if P=∅). A well-described polyhedron is a triplet (P,n,φ) where P has facet complexity ≤φ. Two key lemmas: (1) if P has facet-complexity φ then vertex complexity ≤4n²φ; conversely if vertex complexity υ then φ≤3n²υ; (2) if there is a description of P with facet complexity φ, a standard representation has facet complexity ≤35n²φ. We then connect these notions to the weak/strong oracle framework via four lemmas: if P⊆ℝⁿ is full-dimensional with vertex-complexity υ, all vertices lie in S(0,2^υ) (circumscribing ball); if P has facet complexity ≤φ, then P contains a ball of radius ≥2^{−7n³φ} (inscribed ball); if y∈ℚⁿ with common denominator ≤q and y∈S(P,(1/q)·2^{−2φ}) then y∈P; if a^Tx≤b is an integral inequality almost valid for P (i.e., a^Tx≤b+2^{−(υ+1)} ∀x∈P) then a^Tx≤b ∀x∈P. Summary for well-described polyhedra: (A) the 4 strong problems are equivalent; (B) weak oracles solve the strong problems.

  • Lecture 6.1: Bipartite Matching, Part I We set up the bipartite matching problem on G=(V,E) with V=V₁⊔V₂, E⊆V₁×V₂ (a graph is bipartite iff every cycle has even length). A matching M⊆E has no two edges incident on the same vertex (equivalently Σ{e∈δ(v)} χ^M_e ≤ 1 ∀v∈V). The matching polytope is P_match(G) = conv{χ^M: M a matching} ⊆ ℝ^|E|. The LP relaxation is Q(G) = {x∈ℝ^|E|: 0≤x_e≤1, Σ_{e∈δ(v)} x_e ≤ 1 ∀v}, which satisfies Q(G)⊇P_match(G). Main theorem: if G is bipartite, Q(G) = P_match(G). Proof by induction on |F| where F=supp(x)={e: x_e>0}: Case 1 — F contains a cycle: G bipartite forces the cycle to be even; let d∈{0,+1,−1}^E alternate ±1 along the cycle with supp(d)⊆F=supp(x); for ε small enough x±εd∈Q(G); take ε₁,ε₂ maximally so that x₁=x+ε₁d and x₂=x−ε₂d lie in Q(G) with |supp(x_i)|≤|F|−1; by induction x_i∈P_match(G), so x=λ₁x₁+λ₂x₂∈P_match(G). Consequence: for bipartite G, solving max{Σx_e: x∈Q(G)} gives the value and a certificate of the maximum size matching._

  • Lecture 7.1: Total Unimodularity, Part I We define a matrix A to be totally unimodular (TU) if every square submatrix has determinant in {+1, 0, −1} (entries A{ij}∈{−1,0,+1}; A need not be square). The fundamental integrality theorem: if A is TU (m×n) and b∈ℤᵐ, then P={x: Ax≤b} has integral extreme points. Proof via Cramer’s rule: an extreme point is determined by n tight inequalities (index set B, |B|=n), giving x=A_B^{-1}b_B; by Cramer, x_i = det(A_{Bi}, b_B)/det(A_B) = ±det(A_{Bi}, b_B) ∈ ℤ since TU forces det(A_B)=±1, and (A^{-1}){ij} = det(A{ji}^Δ)/det(A). Duality corollary: if A is TU and b,c are integral, then both the primal min{c^Tx: Ax=b, x≥0} and the dual max{p^Tb: A^Tp≤c} have integral optimal solutions. Key Lemma (T.F.A.E.): (1) A is TU; (2) A^T is TU; (3) [A; I] is TU (this one is circled as most important); (4) [I; A; −A] is TU. Proof of (1)→(3): by expansion by minors, any square submatrix of [A;I] reduces to a square submatrix of A (which has det∈{0,+1,−1} by TU). The result on the previous slide follows from (3) applied to the first theorem. Converse theorem (Integrality implies TUM): if A has independent rows, P={x: Ax=b, x≥0} is integral ∀b integral iff A is TU. Proof strategy: enough to show A_B is TU; A_B TU ⟺ A_B^{-1} integral ⟺ A_B^{-1}v integral ∀ integral v; to verify, choose integral y with z=y+A_B^{-1}v≥0, form z̃=(z;0), show z̃ is an extreme point of an integral polyhedron, hence integral. Hoffman-Kruskal Corollary: A integral {−1,0,1} is TU iff ∀b integral P={x: Ax≤b, x≥0} is integral; proof uses the equivalence A TU ⟺ [A I] TU._

  • Lecture 8.3: Non-Bipartite Matching, Part III This lecture develops the algorithm for finding M-augmenting paths using M-alternating trees. Given matching M, let X⊆V be the set of exposed (unmatched) vertices. An M-alternating tree rooted at r∈X is a tree where every root-to-leaf path alternates between non-matching and matching edges. Vertices are labelled Even (E, at even depth from root — root is E) or Odd (O, at odd depth). Key observation: if the tree reaches a vertex v∈X{r}, the path from r to v is an M-augmenting path. The proof is in three parts. (⇐) Given an M/B-aug path in G/B: if it avoids B the result is immediate; if it passes through B, follow the path until reaching B, then continue through the blossom in whichever of the two directions around the odd cycle maintains alternation — the result is a valid M-aug path in G. (⇒, equivalently M not max ⇒ M/B not max) Take M’=MΔS for an M-augmenting path S; the exposed vertex of M’ lies in B; since B is an odd cycle, the stem is odd length, so |M’|=|M|; in G/B, the entire blossom B corresponds to a single exposed vertex, giving an M’/B-aug path. Two cases for S: if Q avoids B the result is immediate; if Q touches B, since B is contracted to a single exposed vertex in G/B, Q^B (the projection of Q) is an M’/B-aug path in G/B. Edmonds’s Algorithm is then stated in full: (1) construct maximal M-alternating trees — if no E-E edge → M is maximum (Sufficient Condition Lemma); if E-E edge → contract blossom and continue; if aug path found in G/B₁/B₂/…/Bk → recover the aug path in G via repeated application of the lemma; (2) if no aug path and no more E-E edges → stop. Correctness Theorem: when the algorithm terminates, the current matching is maximum — because in the fully contracted graph no E-E edges remain, so by the Sufficient Condition Lemma no aug path exists there, and by the Blossom Lemma no aug path exists in G. The algorithm runs in polynomial time: building M-alternating trees is poly-time; at most |V| blossoms can be contracted before either terminating or finding an aug path; the matching can be augmented at most |V| times.

The primal LP over Q_M is: max Σw_e x_e s.t. x(δ(v))≤1 (dual variable y_v), x(E[U])≤⌊½|U|⌋ for all U⊆V with |U|≥3 odd (dual variable z_U), x≥0. Its dual is: min Σ_v y_v + Σ{|U|≥3} z_U·⌊½|U|⌋ s.t. Proof by induction on |E|+Σw_e (with w_e≥1 ∀e). Case 1: ∃ vertex v̂ present in every max-weight matching — apply inductive hypothesis (exercise). Case 2: every vertex is missed by some max-weight matching — complementary slackness then forces y≡0 in any optimal solution. Choose the optimal z maximizing Σz_U·⌊½|U|⌋²; the support Ω is laminar: if U,W∈Ω cross (U∩W≠∅, U⊄W, W⊄U), one can decrease z_U,z_W by δ=min{z_U,z_W} and increase z_{U∩W},z_{U∪W} by δ, leaving feasibility and optimality unchanged but increasing Σz_U·⌊½|U|⌋² — contradicting maximality. Integrality follows by a shifting argument using laminarity: take inclusion-maximal Û∈Ω with z_Û non-integral; let U₁,…,U_k be the inclusion-maximal subsets of Û in Ω; set z̃Û←z_Û-α, z̃{U_i}←z_{U_i}+α (α=fractional part of z_Û); feasibility is maintained by integrality of w even if k=0, and the objective Σz̃U·⌊½|U|⌋ decreases by laminarity since U_i are disjoint. Three conclusions: (1) ∃ integral dual solution with laminar support; (2) the matching LP is TDI; (3) the Tutte-Berge formula can be recovered from this result (exercise).

The correctness chain w(M) ≥ Σ_{e∈M} Σ_{U: e∈δ(U)} y_U = Σ_U y_U·|M∩δ(U)| ≥ Σ_U y_U (with equality throughout when M is perfect and all CS conditions hold) shows that any feasible dual gives a lower bound, and equality certifies optimality.   • Lecture 8.10: Weighted Matching, Part II We present the weighted matching algorithm in full. The algorithm initializes with Ω = {{v} : v ∈ V} (all singletons), y ≡ 0, and M = ∅. Each iteration builds an M-alternating tree in the quotient graph G’ = G_y/Ω^max, labelling nodes E (even) or O (odd), and proceeds by one of three cases. Case 1: an augmenting path is found in G’; augment M along it and start a new iteration. Case 2: an E-E edge is found in G’; the corresponding odd cycle is a blossom — add the new odd set U to Ω (shrink), update G’, and continue building the tree. Case 3: neither Case 1 nor Case 2 applies; perform a dual update y ← y + θd where d_U = +1 for E-labelled sets U and d_U = −1 for O-labelled sets U, and θ is the largest value keeping dual feasibility. Two events can terminate the θ-update: (A) y_U → 0 for some O-labelled U — de-shrink U by removing it from Ω and expanding it back using its stored Hamiltonian cycle C_U, updating M to give a perfect matching on U; or (B) a new edge becomes tight and is added to E_y, enabling further tree growth. The algorithm terminates when M is a perfect matching in G’ (and hence in G via the stored C_U cycles).

  • Lecture 9.1: Matroids, Part I We introduce matroids. A matroid M = (E, I) consists of a finite ground set E and a nonempty family I of independent sets satisfying: (i) downward closure — if I⊆J and J∈I then I∈I; and (ii) the exchange property — if I,J∈I with |I|<|J|, there exists z∈J\I with I+z∈I. We define the related vocabulary: a base of U⊆E is an inclusion-wise maximal independent subset of U; a key lemma states that any two bases of U have the same cardinality, which allows us to define the rank function r_M(U) = size of a base of U. A circuit is an inclusion-wise minimal dependent set; a loop is a singleton circuit. We also state an equivalent formulation of the exchange property (ii’): if I,J∈I with |I\J|=1 and |J\I|=2, then I+z∈I for some z∈J\I (with (ii)→(ii’) clear and (ii’)→(ii) left as an exercise). We illustrate with five families of matroids: linear matroids (independent sets = linearly independent subsets of vectors v_1,…,v_n), graphic matroids (independent sets = forests of a graph G=(V,E)), binary matroids (linear over GF(2)), uniform matroids (all subsets of cardinality ≤k), partition matroids (I={I⊆E: |I∩E_j|≤k_j ∀j} for a partition E=E_1∪…∪E_ℓ), transversal matroids (independent sets = partial systems of distinct representatives), and matching matroids (I={Z⊆V: Z is covered by some matching in G}).

  • Lecture 10.1: Submodularity, Part I We introduce submodular functions and develop their basic theory. Definition: f:2^E→R is submodular if ∀A,B⊆E: f(A)+f(B)≥f(A∪B)+f(A∩B). Marginal value: f_A(i)=f(A+i)-f(A). Theorem (T.F.A.E.): the following are equivalent: (1) f is submodular; (2) ∀A⊆B⊆E, ∀i∈E\B: f_A(i)≥f_B(i) (diminishing marginal returns); (3) ∀A⊆E, ∀i,j∈E\A: f_A(i)≥f{A+j}(i). We also define monotone: f is monotone if A⊆B⟹f(A)≤f(B). Three closure lemmas follow: (i) α,β≥0 and f,g submodular ⟹ αf+βg submodular; (ii) f submodular and g linear ⟹ f-g submodular; (iii) f,g submodular and f-g monotone ⟹ min(f,g) submodular. We then give four families of examples: (i) rank functions of matroids; (ii) capacity of cuts in a directed graph D=(V,A) with c:A→R+: for fixed r,s∈V and S⊆V\{r,s}, f(S)=c(δ+(S∪{r})) is submodular (exercise); (iii) number of neighbors in a bipartite graph G=(V,E) with V=V_1∪V_2: f(U)=|Γ(U)| is submodular (exercise); (iv) mixed rank of two matroids M_1=(E,I_1), M_2=(E,I_2): f(S)=r_1(S)+r_2(E\S) is submodular — and by the Matroid Intersection Theorem, min_{S⊆E} f(S) = max_{I∈I_1∩I_2} |I|._

  • Lecture 11.3: Lower Bounds, Part I We introduce communication complexity as a tool. A function f:X×Y→{0,1} (X,Y finite) can be encoded as an |X|×|Y| zero-one matrix M(f). The protocol succeeds if: ∃ a mutually acceptable proof whenever f(x,y)=1, and ∄ a mutually acceptable proof when f(x,y)=0. The non-deterministic communication complexity of f is log₂ t, where t is the minimum number of possible hints needed.

First proof (via Yannakakis): supp(S) encodes FV; the r-inequality ext form gives S=UV^T with U,V≥0; then S{f,v}=U_f·V_v>0 iff ∃j∈{1,…,r} s.t. U_{fj}>0 and V_{vj}>0. Protocol: Prover sees f&v and announces index j; Alice accepts iff U_{fj}>0; Bob accepts iff V_{vj}>0. Second proof (via facets of Q): for FV(f,v)=1 (vertex v∉face f), the prover announces a facet G of Q∈{1,…,r} s.t. (i) ∃y_v with (v,y_v)∉G (π^{-1}(v)∩Q⊄G), and (ii) ∀(x,y)∈Q with x∈f, (x,y)∈G (π^{-1}(f)∩Q⊆G). Both Alice and Bob can check conditions (i) and (ii). Such G exists because: π^{-1}(f)∩Q is a face of Q equaling ∩G_{i_k}, and since v∉f the set π^{-1}(v)∩Q does not intersect π^{-1}(f)∩Q, so π^{-1}(v)∩Q cannot be contained in all G_{i_k}, giving at least one G satisfying both conditions. Conclusion: to lower bound xc(P), it suffices to lower bound the non-det comm complexity of FACE-VERTEX(P)._

  • Lecture 11.5: Lower Bounds, Part III We introduce the correlation polytope P_corr^(n) = conv{xx^T: x∈{0,1}^n} ⊆ ℝ^{n²}; its extreme points are y^B:=χ^B(χ^B)^T for B⊆[n]. Theorem: the extension complexity of P_corr^(n) is at least 1.5^n. To apply the FACE-VERTEX framework, we construct a special face F_A for each A⊆[n]. Starting from the valid inequality (Σ{i∈A} x_i−1)²≥0, expanding and substituting y_{ij}=x_ix_j gives: Σ_{i≠j∈A} y_{ij}−Σ_{i∈A} y_{ii}+1≥0, valid for all y∈P_corr. The face F_A is the equality face of this constraint. Lemma: y^B∈F_A iff |A∩B|=1. Using these special faces, we consider FV(A,B): FV(F_A,y^B)=1 iff y^B∉F_A iff |A∩B|≠1. With the promise |A∩B|∈{0,1}, FV(A,B)=1 iff |A∩B|≠1 iff A∩B=∅. The road map then has three steps: ① focus on the subset of the slack matrix corresponding to faces F_A; ② make the problem easier — under the promise, Bob and Alice only need to agree on 1’s in the subset of supp(S) corresponding to A∩B=∅; ③ show that any ext form Q for P_corr with r inequalities implies a covering of D(n)={(A,B)∈2^{[n]}×2^{[n]}: A∩B=∅} of size r (with valid sets). This reduces the ext. complexity lower bound to a combinatorial covering lower bound._

Recap: support of slack matrix encodes FV; focusing on faces F_A and vertices y^B, FV(A,B)=1 iff |A∩B|≠1; with promise |A∩B|∈{0,1}, FV(A,B)=1 iff A∩B=∅.   • Lecture 11.7: Lower Bounds, Part V We prove the final lower bound. Recall: xc(P_corr^(n)) ≥ size of min valid covering of D(n), where D(n)={(A,B)∈2^{[n]}×2^{[n]}: A∩B=∅}. Lemma 1: |D(n)|=3^n (each element can be in A only, B only, or neither). Lemma 2: any covering of D(n) by valid sets requires at least 1.5^n sets. Proof: let p(n)=max cardinality of a valid subset of D(n); we show p(n)≤2^n. Then D(n)⊆R₁∪…∪R_r with each R_i valid implies r·p(n)≥|D(n)|=3^n, so r≥3^n/2^n=1.5^n. To prove p(n)≤2p(n-1) by induction: given R⊆D(n), define R₁=({(A,B)∈R: n∈A}∪{(A,B)∈R: (A∪{n},B)∉R})∩([n]×[n-1]) and R₂=({(A,B)∈R: n∈B}∪{(A,B)∈R: (A,B∪{n})∉R})∩([n-1]×[n]). Define π_n:R→D(n-1) by (A,B)↦(A\{n},B\{n}). R valid ⟹ R₁,R₂ valid (both ⊆R). Claim: R₁ valid ⟹ π_n(R₁) valid — since R₁⊆[n]×[n-1], n∉B’ for any (A’,B’)∈R₁, so |A∩n ∩ B’\n|=|·|≠1; similarly π_n(R₂) valid. π_n is injective on R₁ and R₂ by their definitions (each element of [n-1]×[n-1] has a unique preimage in R₁ and R₂). Thus |R₁|+|R₂|=|π_n(R₁)|+|π_n(R₂)|≤2p(n-1). Since R⊆R₁∪R₂ by construction, |R|≤2p(n-1)≤2^n. Hence p(n)≤2^n and xc(P_corr^(n))≥1.5^n.
